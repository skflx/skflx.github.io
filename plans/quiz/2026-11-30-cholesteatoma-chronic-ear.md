## 4. Chapters

### 1. Chronic otitis media and tympanic membrane perforation (step A, ~30 min, `src`: `C2 · P16 · J5 · K14 · K15 · K17 · K18 · E33–E45 · E53`)

File: `js/oksat/guides/cholesteatoma/com-tm.js` (`id: 'com-tm'`, `k: 1`, `short: 'COM & tympanoplasty'`, `min: 30`, `sections: 4`).

TL;DR:
- Chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM) is middle ear discharge through a TM perforation lasting >3 months; granulation tissue alone destroys bone via collagenase even without cholesteatoma (`K14`), and chronic ear infections shift toward *Proteus*, *Pseudomonas*, and *Enterococcus* (`K15`).
- Posterior and marginal perforations carry more conductive hearing loss (phase cancellation over the round window) and higher cholesteatoma risk because marginal defects lack the fibrous annulus (`J5`, `E35`, `E36`).
- In COM without cholesteatoma, tympanoplasty alone matches tympanoplasty plus mastoidectomy (`E53`); underlay temporalis fascia is standard (`E40`), lateral grafting risks anterior blunting and lateralization (`J5`), and myringoplasty is contraindicated in an only-hearing ear (`E40`).
- In children, a **healthy contralateral ear** predicts tympanoplasty success better than age alone; elective repair is often delayed until around age 10, though earlier surgery is reasonable for troublesome otorrhea or hearing loss (`P16`).

#### 1.1 Chronic OM, TM structure, and Eustachian tube anatomy (concept `com-et-anatomy`, `ref`: `Otology/Eustachian tube dysfunction divides into dilatory and patulous forms with opposite treatment goals`, `Otology/Patulous Eustachian tube manifests with autophony, respiratory-synchronous tinnitus, and tympanic membrane excursions on nasal breathing`)

- Claims:
  - Chronic suppurative otitis media is defined clinically as chronic middle ear discharge through a tympanic membrane perforation (>3 months, versus an inactive dry perforation) `[K14]`.
  - Silent otitis media (Paparella et al., 1980) denotes chronic middle ear inflammatory pathology occurring behind an intact TM without perforation or otorrhea `[K14]`.
  - Granulation tissue in chronic OM can destroy bone (90% collagen) via collagenase enzymatic activity even without cholesteatoma `[K14]`.
  - Cholesterol granuloma consists of granulation tissue with cholesterol crystals and giant cells caused by bleeding in a poorly aerated cavity `[K14]`.
  - Unlike acute OM, chronic OM complications most commonly involve *Proteus mirabilis*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, and *Enterococcus* `[K15]`.
  - Unlike the three-layered pars tensa, the pars flaccida is deficient in its central fibrous layer `[J5]`.
  - The vascular strip in the posterior-superior external auditory canal supplies blood to the central portion of the tympanic membrane `[J5]`.
  - The long process of the incus has a single blood supply without collaterals, predisposing it to erosion in chronic ear disease `[C2]`.
  - The Eustachian tube is two-thirds cartilaginous and one-third bony, with the tensor veli palatini serving as its primary dilator `[C2]`.
  - Rapid weight loss can atrophy Ostmann's fat pad in the Eustachian tube, resulting in patulous Eustachian tube syndrome `[C2]`.
  - The Eustachian tube is 18 mm long and horizontal at birth, lengthening to 35 mm with a 45-degree inclination in adults `[K14]`; aging from childhood to adulthood increases slope, length, diameter, and opening efficiency `[C2]`.
  - During balloon Eustachian tuboplasty, advancement of the balloon must stop once resistance is met at the bony isthmus `[J5]`.

#### 1.2 Tympanic membrane perforations and retraction pockets (concept `perforations-retractions`, `ref`: `Otology/Traumatic tympanic membrane perforations heal spontaneously in the majority of cases without early surgery`)

- Claims:
  - Conductive hearing loss from TM perforation affects low frequencies more, and for equal-sized perforations, posterior perforations cause greater hearing loss than anterior ones (though current evidence from Merchant and Rosowski shows perforation size, not location, determines the loss) `[E36]`.
  - Posterior marginal TM perforations overlying the round window produce greater conductive hearing loss due to phase cancellation `[J5]`.
  - Marginal perforations lack the fibrous annulus and are considered "unsafe" because EAC skin can easily advance into the middle ear to form cholesteatoma `[E35]`.
  - An overlying blood clot on a posttraumatic TM perforation should not be removed because it guides spontaneous healing `[E40]`.
  - Topographically, 60% of TM retraction pockets occur in the pars tensa (36% posterior-superior, 16% inferior) and 40% in the pars flaccida `[K18]`.
  - In children with middle ear inflammation, mesenchyme in the TM lamina propria (normally resorbed in a few months) persists up to 3 years, impairing elastin maturation and promoting retraction pockets `[K18]`.
  - Sadé (1979) five-grade atelectasis / pars tensa retraction classification: Grade I mild retraction; Grade II contacts incus or stapes `[E33]`; Grade III contacts promontory without adhesion; Grade IV adherent to promontory; Grade V spontaneous perforation with otorrhea/polyps `[K18]`.
  - In the Tos classification of pars flaccida retraction, Type IV shows distinct bone resorption of the osseous annulus with retraction extending to the malleus head `[K18]`.
  - Patients with myringostapedopexy generally maintain good hearing, so surgery is not indicated unless conductive hearing loss or frequent otorrhea develops `[E33]`.
  - Long-term conservative treatment with ventilation tubes does not alter the further evolution of atelectasis `[E33]`.
  - Cartilage-perichondrial tympanoplasty for severe retraction pockets achieved 0% graft failure in grafted areas, though 13% developed new pockets elsewhere `[K18]`.

  - `call` (`pearl`, "Leave the traumatic clot"): an overlying clot acts as a healing scaffold on a posttraumatic perforation `[E40]`.

#### 1.3 Tympanoplasty techniques, grafts, and technical pitfalls (concept `tympanoplasty-technique`, `ref`: `Otology/Medial underlay fascia grafting avoids anterior blunting and lateral graft displacement`, `Otology/Underlay and overlay tympanoplasty techniques have distinct advantages and complication profiles`, `Otology/Cartilage island tympanoplasty resists negative middle ear pressure and recurrent retraction`, `Otology/Cartilage palisade and butterfly inlay tympanoplasty techniques reinforce the reconstructed tympanic membrane against eustachian tubal negative pressure while preserving acoustic transmission`)

- Claims:
  - Microsurgical repair of chronic TM perforation achieves well over 90% success in experienced hands `[J5]`; reperforation occurs in ~5% of myringoplasties, with revision indicated after a few months `[E41]`.
  - In chronic OM without cholesteatoma, tympanoplasty alone yields the same graft failure rate and postoperative hearing status as tympanoplasty with mastoidectomy `[E53]`.
  - Perforations posterior to the malleus can be approached transcanally, whereas anterior perforations are best approached postauricularly for visualization `[J5]`.
  - Anterior TM perforations without a remaining rim have a higher reperforation rate unless specialized techniques are used `[J5]`; when only a small anterior TM residue remains, an overlay technique placing the graft over the annulus prevents anterior graft detachment `[E39]`.
  - Underlay technique using autologous temporalis fascia is preferred for most myringoplasties for superior anatomical and functional results `[E40]`.
  - In lateral graft tympanoplasty, the fascia graft is positioned **lateral to the TM remnant but medial to the malleus handle** `[J5]`.
  - Autologous cartilage grafts (tragal or conchal) are used to prepare for ossiculoplasty and because the neomembrane better resists reperforation `[J5]`.
  - Butterfly cartilage tympanoplasty is best suited for small- and medium-sized posterior or central perforations; for round perforations, cartilage is cut with a dermatological punch **1 mm wider** than the measured perforation `[J5]`.
  - Large areas of myringosclerosis / dense tympanosclerotic plaques should be removed during tympanoplasty because they impair drum vibration and graft vascularity `[J5, E39]`.

### 2. Cholesteatoma: congenital vs acquired, pathogenesis, staging, and imaging (step A, ~35 min, `src`: `C2 · P16 · J8 · K16 · E35 · E43 · E46–E48 · E52 · E56`)

File: `js/oksat/guides/cholesteatoma/cholesteatoma-dx.js` (`id: 'cholesteatoma-dx'`, `k: 2`, `short: 'Cholesteatoma & imaging'`, `min: 35`, `sections: 4`).

TL;DR:
- Congenital cholesteatoma (2–7%) is an epidermoid cyst behind an intact TM with no prior otorrhea, perforation, or ear surgery (prior AOM does not exclude it); the board-keyed site is anterosuperior (`J8`, `K16`, `E56`), and early-stage recurrence is 0–13% vs up to two-thirds in advanced disease (`K16`).
- Acquired cholesteatoma arises from retraction pockets (pars flaccida in adults, but **2× more often pars tensa in children**, causing early incus/stapes erosion) or migration through marginal perforations, driven by elevated Ki-67/MIB-1, reduced apoptosis, and MMPs (`J8`, `K16`, `P16`, `E35`).
- Growth follows embryologic mucosal folds: posterior epitympanic spreads **lateral** to the ossicles, while posterior mesotympanic penetrates toward the mastoid **medial** to the ossicles (`J8`).
- Diagnosis is clinical; noncontrast temporal bone CT is for surgical planning and red-flag complications (`E52`, `K16`, `C2`), while non-EPI DWI (HASTE) MRI detects residual/recurrent keratin as bright restricted diffusion and reduces second-look surgery (`K16`, `P16`).

#### 2.1 Congenital cholesteatoma: definition, site, and staging (concept `congenital-cholesteatoma`, `ref`: `Otology/Congenital cholesteatoma presents as an anterosuperior white mass behind an intact tympanic membrane`)

- Claims:
  - Cholesteatoma is an epidermal inclusion cyst in the middle ear with a stratified squamous epithelium capsule/matrix and concentrically accumulating keratin `[E46]`.

#### 2.2 Acquired cholesteatoma: pathogenesis, biology, and pediatric vs adult behavior (concept `acquired-pathogenesis`, `ref`: `Otology/Primary acquired cholesteatoma arises from retraction pocket invagination of the pars flaccida into Prussak space`, `Otology/Secondary acquired cholesteatoma develops from squamous epithelial migration through a tympanic membrane perforation`, `Otology/The epitympanic diaphragm and tympanic isthmus govern attic ventilation and selective epitympanic dysventilation`)

### 3. Surgery: CWU vs CWD, surveillance, recidivism, and ossiculoplasty (step B, ~40 min, `src`: `C2 · P16 · J7 · J8 · K16 · K17 · E41 · E44 · E47 · E48 · E51 · E53–E56`)

TL;DR:
- Residual cholesteatoma is squamous epithelium left behind (most often under the tympanic CN VII, around the stapes/oval window, facial recess, and sinus tympani); recurrent cholesteatoma is a new retraction pocket forming de novo (`J8`).
- In adults, CWD yields lower recidivism than CWU (up to 20% in CWU) and is favored for only-hearing ear, large fistula, bilateral disease, or severe hypopneumatization (`P16`, `K16`, `J8`); in children, **CWU is favored (~90%)** because children tolerate open cavities poorly and cortical bone regrows, and `P16` explicitly rejects "mandatory CWD" rules for children (`P16`).
- Second-look CWU timing is a range—earlier in children (6 months children vs 6–18 months adults, `K17`); non-EPI DWI reduces second-stage surgery (`P16`), and CWU requires ≥10 years of otoscopic follow-up (`E53`).
- Avoid a wet CWD cavity by widening the meatus, lowering the facial ridge (leaving 2–3 mm bone over CN VII), beveling overhangs, and amputating a prominent mastoid tip (`E54`, `J8`); pediatric mastoid/attic obliteration drops 5-year revision from 15–20% to 2–3% (`P16`).
- Autologous incus interposition is preferred (75% normal hearing in children, `P16`, `E51`); adult PORP hits ≤15 dB and TORP ≤25 dB in two-thirds of patients, and cartilage interposition halves hydroxyapatite extrusion from 7.9% to 3.7% (`K17`).

#### 4.3 Labyrinthine fistula (concept `labyrinthine-fistula`, `ref`: `Otology/Labyrinthine fistula from cholesteatoma erosions most frequently involves the lateral semicircular canal`, `Otology/Management of a labyrinthine fistula requires preservation of the matrix until complete bony exposure`)

- Claims:
  - Labyrinthine fistula occurs in **~7–10%** of cholesteatomas, with the **horizontal/lateral semicircular canal** accounting for **90%** of fistulae `[K15]`.
  - Labyrinthine fistulas are more common in **poorly pneumatized temporal bones** because dense lateral bone directs erosive force medially to the otic capsule `[J8]`.
  - **CT detects labyrinthine fistulae accurately in only 57–60% of suspected cases**, so surgeons must **presume a fistula in every cholesteatoma operation** `[K15]`.
  - Whenever a semicircular canal fistula occurs, the **tympanic segment of the facial nerve is also frequently eroded** and requires extra care `[J8]`.
  - In **open-cavity (CWD)** surgery with a labyrinthine fistula, cholesteatoma matrix is generally **left over the fistula**, whereas in **closed-cavity (CWU)** cases it is **removed with a 6-month second look** `[E52]`. In an **only-hearing ear** or with a **large fistula**, the matrix is left on the fistula (open cavity).
  - If a semicircular canal lumen is entered during surgery, **potentially ototoxic ear drops must be avoided on the packing** `[J8]`.

### `case-retraction-cholesteatoma` (Paired peds vs adult — acquired cholesteatoma & CWU vs CWD)
- **Vignette (raw findings):** Two patients present with foul-smelling otorrhea and conductive hearing loss. **Patient A** is a 4-year-old boy with a deep posterosuperior pars tensa retraction pocket containing keratin debris, absent distal incus long process, and small, poorly pneumatized mastoid air cells on noncontrast temporal bone CT. **Patient B** is a 52-year-old man with bilateral attic retraction pockets eroding both scuta, severely hypopneumatized sclerotic mastoids, and only a 10-dB conductive gap on the side with extensive ossicular erosion.
- **`q44` (Q1 Interpret, MCQ, correct `b`):** Why does Patient A already have ossicular erosion at age 4, and how should his small mastoid volume be interpreted?
  - Keyed (`b`): Pediatric acquired cholesteatoma is twice as likely to arise from the pars tensa as the pars flaccida (causing early incus/stapes erosion even at ages 3–4), and underdeveloped mastoid volume is the norm in pediatric cholesteatoma `[P16]`.
  - Distractors: (`a`) Extensive erosion at age 4 proves congenital cholesteatoma (`P16` states acquired is much more common and 3–4-year-olds present with bone erosion), (`c`) Children normally have highly cellular mastoids, so hypopneumatization rules out primary cholesteatoma (the `E47`/`E48` conflict overridden by `P16`), (`d`) Pars flaccida pockets in children erode the malleus first before reaching the incus (inverts pars tensa 2× ratio `[P16]`).
  - Pearl: In children, pars tensa retraction drives early incus/stapes erosion inside an underdeveloped mastoid. Discrepancy: `P16` (keyed) documents smaller mastoid volume; `E47`/`E48` claim highly cellular pediatric mastoids.
- **`q45` (Q2 Integrate, MCQ, correct `d`):** Why does Patient B perceive minimal hearing loss despite incus erosion, and what was the proper indication for obtaining CT in both patients?
  - Keyed (`d`): The cholesteatoma mass transmits sound across the ossicular defect; diagnosis is clinical, and CT is obtained for surgical planning or suspected complications `[K16, E52]`.
  - Distractors: (`a`) Phase cancellation over the round window cancels out ossicular discontinuity; CT is mandatory to diagnose every cholesteatoma (misapplies `[J5]` and contradicts `[E52, K16]`), (`b`) Tympanosclerotic plaques stiffen the chain to preserve hearing; CT excludes tympanosclerosis because both erode the scutum (contradicts `[E43]`), (`c`) Intact stapes footplate alone prevents >10 dB loss; contrast MRI should have preceded CT even without red flags (contradicts `[E52]`).
  - Pearl: A "cholesteatoma hearer" has preserved hearing only because the sac bridges the gap—and CT maps the anatomy for surgery rather than making the diagnosis.
- **`q46` (Q3 Choose, MCQ, correct `a` — NAIVE WRONG ANSWER):** Both patients have reliable follow-up. What is the best primary surgical strategy for Patient A (4-year-old) versus Patient B (52-year-old with bilateral disease and severely hypopneumatized mastoids)?
  - Keyed (`a`): Staged CWU (considering mastoid/attic obliteration to cut 5-year revision from 15–20% to 2–3%) for Patient A; primary CWD for Patient B `[P16, J8, K16]`.
  - **Naive wrong answer (`c`):** Primary CWD for both patients because underdeveloped mastoids and bone erosion mandate exteriorization in all ages (applies adult CWD logic to a 4-year-old, ignoring pediatric cavity intolerance, GA debridement, and cortical bone regrowth `[P16]`).
  - Other distractors: (`b`) Primary CWU for Patient B and modified Bondy for Patient A (ignores high ICW recurrence in adult bilateral/hypopneumatized disease `[J8]` and Bondy requires intact ossicles/TM `[E55]`), (`d`) Atticotomy alone for both patients (disease extends beyond Prussak space `[K16]`).
  - Pearl: Same CT appearance, opposite canal wall choices—children get CWU (with obliteration if indicated) because they tolerate cavities poorly and regrow cortical bone; adults with bilateral sclerotic disease get primary CWD.

### `case-labyrinthine-fistula` (Paired peds vs adult — lateral semicircular canal fistula & bilateral cholesteatoma)
- **Vignette (raw findings):** Two patients with chronic cholesteatoma report episodic vertigo. **Patient A** is a 9-year-old girl with **bilateral cholesteatoma**; her family is highly reliable. **Patient B** is a 61-year-old man with normal contralateral hearing and a severely hypopneumatized sclerotic temporal bone. Both preoperative noncontrast temporal bone CTs are read as "intact otic capsule."
- **`q47` (Q1 Interpret, MCQ, correct `c`):** How do you interpret the normal otic-capsule CT reports, which structure is most likely eroded, and what companion structure must you expect to be exposed?
  - Keyed (`c`): CT detects only 57–60% of fistulae (so presume one in both); 90% involve the lateral semicircular canal, often with tympanic CN VII erosion `[K15, J8]`.
  - Distractors: (`a`) Normal HRCT excludes a fistula, pointing instead to otitic hydrocephalus (contradicts 57–60% `[K15]`), (`b`) CT detects >95% of fistulae; 90% involve the superior semicircular canal, and the labyrinthine facial nerve is usually bare alongside the erosion (wrong canal & nerve segment `[K15, J8]`), (`d`) Fistulae occur predominantly in hyperpneumatized mastoids where cells open into the vestibule (inverts `[J8]`).
  - Pearl: A "normal" CT misses ~40% of lateral canal fistulae—operate expecting both a canal fistula and a bare tympanic facial nerve.
- **`q48` (Q2 Integrate, MCQ, correct `b` — NAIVE WRONG ANSWER):** Intraoperatively, both patients have a lateral semicircular canal fistula. How does canal-wall and matrix management differ between Patient A (9-year-old, bilateral cholesteatoma) and Patient B (61-year-old, sclerotic mastoid)?
  - Keyed (`b`): For Patient A, CWD is not mandatory in children (CWU removes matrix with a 6-month second look) (`E52`, `P16`); for Patient B, CWD is favored and matrix is left on the fistula (`K16`, `E52`).
  - **Naive wrong answer (`a`):** Mandatory primary CWD for Patient A because bilateral disease and fistula are absolute CWD indications in every patient, with matrix stripped in Patient B as well (direct trap on Conflict #1: `P16` explicitly disagrees for children).
  - Other distractors: (`c`) Leave matrix permanently over the fistula inside a closed CWU cavity in Patient A (violates `[E52]`—matrix is removed in closed cavities), (`d`) Perform modified Bondy in both because the labyrinth is at risk (Bondy requires intact TM/ossicles and lateral attic disease only `[E55]`).
  - Pearl: Know Conflict #1 cold—in adults, only-hearing ear and large fistula favor CWD (leaving matrix over the fistula); in children, `P16` explicitly rejects mandatory CWD. Discrepancy: `K16`/`J8`/`E48` (adults) vs `P16` (children).
- **`q49` (Q3 Choose, recall):** During dissection in Patient A, the lateral semicircular canal lumen is inadvertently entered. State the immediate packing precaution, the surveillance options over the next 6–12 months, and the required long-term clinical follow-up duration after CWU.
  - Answer: • Avoid potentially ototoxic ear drops on the packing once the semicircular canal lumen is entered `[J8]` • Perform a planned 6-month second look after closed-cavity fistula matrix removal (`E52`, `K17` pediatric interval) and/or use non-EPI DWI (HASTE) MRI surveillance (`P16`, `K16`) • Maintain regular otoscopic follow-up for at least 10 years after CWU `[E53]`.
  - Pearl: Opened canal = zero ototoxic drops on the packing; closed cavity = 6-month pediatric look plus 10-year otoscopy.

### `case-mastoiditis-complications` (Paired peds vs adult — acute mastoiditis, abscess tracking, and LST)
- **Vignette (raw findings):** **Patient A** is an 18-month-old boy with a right cochlear implant who presents with fever, a fluctuant postauricular swelling pushing the pinna laterally and inferiorly, and maximal tenderness over the fossa mastoidea. **Patient B** is a 48-year-old man with chronic otorrhea who develops tender swelling deep to the sternocleidomastoid, spiking "picket fence" fevers, and postauricular edema/tenderness over the mastoid emissary region.
- **`q50` (Q1 Interpret, MCQ, correct `d`):** What anatomical pathways explain Patient A's postauricular abscess and Patient B's neck abscess, and what surgical risk is unique to Patient A's age if a postauricular incision is made?

  - Distractors: (`a`) Both abscesses exited through the notch of Rivinus; CN VII is protected by thick infantile cortical bone (wrong anatomy `[C2, J7]`), (`b`) Patient A has a Bezold abscess tracking lateral to SCM; Patient B has Gradenigo syndrome (swaps diagnoses `[K15]`), (`c`) Patient A's tenderness sits over the mastoid tip; Patient B's abscess tracks via newborn cribriform channels (inverts adult vs child `[K15]`).
  - Pearl: Cribriform channels + fossa mastoidea tenderness + superficial CN VII = infant mastoiditis; medial tip perforation under SCM = adult Bezold abscess.
- **`q51` (Q2 Integrate, MCQ, correct `a`):** Patient B's picket-fence fever and Griesinger sign point to which intracranial complication, what imaging confirms it, and what extra diagnostic workup would be essential if Patient A (a child) developed the same complication?
  - Keyed (`a`): Lateral sinus thrombosis; contrast-enhanced CT (empty delta sign) plus MRI/MRV; in children, work up underlying prothrombotic disorders (present in 5 of 7 children in series) `[K15, E52]`.
  - Distractors: (`b`) Subdural empyema; immediate lumbar puncture before imaging; no hematologic testing in children (LP contraindicated `[K16]` and misses pediatric thrombophilia `[K15]`), (`c`) Otitic hydrocephalus; noncontrast CT showing dilated ventricles; optic nerve decompression immediately (ventricles are non-enlarged `[K16]`), (`d`) Meningitis; noncontrast CT alone is sufficient (contrast + MRI needed for intracranial complications `[E52]`).
  - Pearl: Griesinger sign (emissary vein thrombosis) + picket-fence fever → contrast CT (empty delta) + MRI, and in children always order a thrombophilia panel.
- **`q52` (Q3 Choose, MCQ, correct `c`):** What is the initial management of Patient A's postauricular subperiosteal abscess over his cochlear implant?
  - Keyed (`c`): Early I&D, myringotomy with ventilation tube placement, and IV antibiotics, which virtually always saves the implant `[P16]`.
  - Distractors: (`a`) Immediate cochlear implant explantation to clear biofilm, followed by canal wall down mastoidectomy and oral antibiotics alone (contradicts device salvage `[P16]`), (`b`) Outpatient oral antibiotics and observation for 72 hours (inadequate for subperiosteal abscess `[P16]`), (`d`) Immediate canal wall down mastoidectomy with implant removal (unwarranted `[P16]`).
  - Pearl: Act early with I&D, myringotomy/tube, and IV antibiotics—the pediatric cochlear implant is virtually always saved.

### `case-congenital-tympanoplasty-ocr` (Pediatric congenital cholesteatoma, tympanoplasty timing & OCR)
- **Vignette (raw findings):** A 3-year-old girl has an asymptomatic pearly white anterosuperior mass behind an intact TM; she had two episodes of AOM at age 1, no otorrhea, no perforation, and no ear surgery.   - Distractors: (`a`) Prior AOM excludes congenital cholesteatoma, requiring staged CWU with 67% recurrence (contradicts `[E56, K16]`), (`c`) Congenital cholesteatoma classically arises in the posterior mesotympanum and requires CWD (wrong board site—`E56` series caveat never keyed—and wrong surgery `[J8, K16, E56]`), (`d`) Observation until age 10 is recommended because early lesions involute like Michaels' structure (no spontaneous involution postnatally; delay raises recurrence up to 2/3 `[K16, J8]`).
  - Pearl: Prior AOM is allowed in congenital cholesteatoma; early extended tympanotomy keeps recurrence at 0–13% instead of up to two-thirds.
- **`q54` (Q2 Integrate, MCQ, correct `d`):** How should you counsel the parents of the 6-year-old brother on tympanoplasty timing and his strongest positive prognostic factor?

  - Distractors: (`a`) Perform immediate myringoplasty under local anesthesia because age never affects pediatric closure (contradicts `P16` age/GA `[E40]`), (`b`) Wait strictly until age 15 regardless of otorrhea because contralateral ear status does not predict closure (invents rigid cut-off and contradicts `[P16]`), (`c`) Add cortical mastoidectomy to tympanoplasty because his perforation followed a tube (contradicts `[E53]`).
  - Pearl: Delay elective repair in asymptomatic young children, operate sooner for troublesome drainage or hearing loss, and trust the healthy contralateral ear over any single birthday. Discrepancy: `P16` (keyed) vs `K17` (5–7 yrs for isolated OCR) vs `E40` (regardless of age).
- **`q55` (Q3 Choose, recall):** Suppose the 3-year-old had instead presented at age 9 with a 3-mm distal incus erosion and an intact mobile stapes.   - Pearl: Over 2 mm gap → sculpted autologous incus first in children (75% normal hearing), and always cap an alloplastic prosthesis with cartilage.
